Friendly service is a wonderful foundation, and this client was quick to give credit for it. But warmth at the front of the house can't make up for a bill that lands well above the salon's own menu, paired with a result that didn't match what was asked for. This report on Advanced Nails is a clear study in two things clients rarely forgive: being overcharged against posted prices, and being promised a look that isn't delivered.

## Charged Well Above the Listed Prices

The client pointed to the salon's own printed prices. The pedicure was listed in the booklet at roughly $45 to $55, and she was charged $75. The manicure was supposed to be around $55, and she was charged $80. A salon's own menu is a promise, and when the final total sits well above it with no explanation, the client is left feeling the numbers were adjusted at the register. That feeling is only sharpened when, as here, the quality didn't justify even the listed price, let alone the higher one.

## "Nail Art" Advertised, but Not Delivered

There was also a gap between what the salon advertised and what it produced. A sign in the store promoted "nail art," which the client reasonably took to mean the skill was there. She came in with a specific request, but the result didn't match it, a difference clear enough that she documented what she asked for next to what she received. Advertising a specialty sets an expectation, and when the work falls short of it, the disappointment is bigger than if nothing had been promised at all.

## Editorial Perspective

The encouraging part is that the hardest thing to teach, genuine, warm customer service, is already in place. The gaps are in pricing integrity and matching skill to the promise, and both are fixable. First, honor your posted prices: charge what the menu says, and if a design or add-on costs more, quote that clearly before starting, never spring it at checkout. Second, only advertise what you can reliably deliver. If "nail art" is on the sign, the team needs to be able to execute a client's reference, or to set expectations honestly about what's achievable. Third, make quality match the price. A warm greeting can't offset a set that disappoints at a premium cost. Align the bill with the menu and the work with the promise, and this salon's already-strong hospitality would carry it the rest of the way.
