Decades after it began transforming the plains, no-till farming continues to help Kansas growers stretch scarce water and protect the fragile soils that define much of the state.

By leaving crop residue on the surface instead of plowing it under, no-till reduces evaporation, curbs erosion, and gradually builds organic matter. In the semi-arid west, where every inch of rain counts, the moisture savings can mean the difference between a crop and a failure.

"No-till is the reason we can farm ground out here that used to blow away," a producer near the High Plains said. The practice, now widespread, requires specialized equipment and careful weed management.

Researchers continue to refine the approach, studying how it interacts with cover crops and rotations to make dryland farming more resilient.
