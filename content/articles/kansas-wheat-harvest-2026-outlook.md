Combines began rolling through the golden fields of Kansas this month as the winter wheat harvest got underway, opening a season that farmers describe as a patchwork of hope and disappointment depending on where the rain fell.

Kansas is consistently among the top wheat-producing states in the country, and the crop remains a defining feature of its identity and economy. This year, timely spring rains blessed parts of central Kansas with promising fields, while stretches of the drier west again battled thin stands and stressed grain.

"Drive an hour in any direction and you will see a completely different crop," said an agronomist with a grain cooperative. Custom harvest crews, the migratory teams that follow the ripening wheat north from Texas, are once again a common sight on rural highways.

### Weather is the wild card

As always, the biggest threats now are hail and untimely rain that can knock down or sprout mature grain before it can be cut. Elevators across the state are preparing for the rush of trucks that will line up over the coming weeks, a seasonal rhythm that has defined rural Kansas for more than a century.
