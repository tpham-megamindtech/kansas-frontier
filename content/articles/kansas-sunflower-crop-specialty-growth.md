Fields of nodding yellow blooms are becoming a more common late-summer sight in Kansas, as growers expand sunflower acreage to meet demand for cooking oil and confection seeds in the state that bears the flower's name.

Sunflowers are well suited to the Kansas climate, tolerating heat and drought better than some crops and offering farmers a rotation option that breaks pest and disease cycles. The seeds are pressed for oil or bagged as snacks, and the crop draws tourists who flock to photograph the fields at peak bloom.

"It is a practical crop that also happens to be beautiful," a grower in north-central Kansas said. Processors have expanded contracts, giving farmers a reliable market.

Agronomists caution that sunflowers attract their own pests, but say the crop's resilience makes it an appealing fit for the state's variable conditions.
